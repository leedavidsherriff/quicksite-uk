Showers and wet rooms that don't leak

Tanked walls and floors, falls to the drain and tiling built for daily soaking.

Get my guide priceCall 07925 753336

A shower or wet room is the hardest-working tiled area in any house, and the one where shortcuts cause the most damage. Water that gets behind tiles can rot floors, stain ceilings below and grow mould in walls long before you see it. We tile walk-in showers, shower enclosures and full wet rooms for homeowners in Wigan, building in proper waterproofing from the floor to above the shower head. Whether you want a level-access wet room for easier living or a walk-in shower with a glass screen, we'll get the details right underneath the tiles.

Tanking: the part you never see

Tanking means making the area watertight before tiling. The walls around the shower are lined with cement-based or waterproof insulation boards rather than standard plasterboard, then coated with a liquid waterproofing membrane or sheet system. Corners and board joints are reinforced with waterproof tape, and pipe entries and valves get sealing collars. In a wet room, the whole floor and the lower part of the walls are tanked, with a lip or upstand at the doorway. The membrane must cure fully before tiling. Done properly, tanking means the tiles are decoration and the waterproofing does the real work.

Falls, drains and floor formers

A wet room floor must fall gently towards the drain so water runs away instead of pooling or escaping out of the room. The usual approach is a pre-formed wet room tray, or former, set into the floor, which gives a built-in fall and an integrated drain. On timber floors the joists may need to be notched or the floor built up, which needs checking to make sure the floor stays strong. Linear drains allow large tiles to fall in one direction, while centre drains usually need smaller tiles or mosaics to follow the four-way slope. Getting the falls right is critical and hard to fix later.

Choosing tiles for wet areas

Shower floors need slip resistance. Look for tiles with a good slip rating, or use small mosaics where the extra grout lines add grip underfoot. Porcelain is ideal as it absorbs almost no water. Natural stone like marble and slate looks beautiful but needs sealing and more careful cleaning, and some stones react to acidic cleaners. On walls, large-format tiles mean fewer grout joints to clean. Epoxy or high-performance grout resists staining and mould better than standard grout. Recessed niches for shampoo and soap are best planned early, so they can be tanked and lined up with the tile joints.

How long it takes

Typically 4–7 days for a wet room, including tanking cure times

Questions about showers and wet rooms

What is the difference between a wet room and a walk-in shower?

A walk-in shower usually has a low-profile tray and a glass screen, with only the shower area waterproofed. A wet room is fully tanked across the whole floor and lower walls, and the floor itself falls to a drain, with no tray. Wet rooms need more preparation and are usually more expensive, but they are fully level-access.

Can you build a wet room on a timber floor?

Yes, in most cases. The existing boards are usually replaced with structural ply or a wet room former, and the joists may need to be adjusted to set the former and drain at the right height. We'll check the joists' size and condition first. Upstairs wet rooms need particular care, as any leak will show in the ceiling below.

Why is my shower leaking through the ceiling?

The most common causes are failed silicone around the tray, cracked or missing grout, a leaking waste or valve, or walls that were never tanked. Water soaks through grout into plasterboard over time. Replacing silicone can help temporarily, but if the walls weren't tanked, the only lasting fix is stripping out and re-tiling with proper waterproofing.
