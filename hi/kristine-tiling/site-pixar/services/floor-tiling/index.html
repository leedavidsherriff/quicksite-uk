A tiled floor should last for decades, but only if what's underneath is right. Cracked tiles, hollow spots and grout breaking up are almost always caused by a floor that moves, a subfloor that wasn't levelled or adhesive that didn't fully cover the back of the tile. We tile kitchens, hallways, utility rooms, bathrooms and open-plan living spaces for homeowners in Wigan, onto concrete, screed and timber floors. We'll check the base, explain what preparation it needs and give you a quote that shows it clearly, so there are no surprises once the old floor is up.

Concrete, screed and timber bases

Concrete and sand-cement screed floors are ideal for tiling once they're fully dry, clean and level. New screeds need time to dry, roughly a day per millimetre for the first 40 mm, and anhydrite screeds need sanding and priming. Timber floors flex, so tiles laid straight onto floorboards will crack. They are normally overlaid with screwed exterior-grade plywood, or tile backer boards, to stiffen them, and a decoupling membrane can be added to absorb movement. Uneven floors are levelled with a self-levelling compound. We'll check the floor's condition and flatness before quoting. A long straightedge laid across the floor shows up dips and humps quickly, and we check it in several directions.

Choosing tiles for floors

Porcelain is the go-to choice for most floors because it's hard, dense, frost-resistant and needs no sealing. Ceramic floor tiles are cheaper but less hard-wearing, and suit bathrooms more than busy kitchens. Natural stone such as slate, limestone and travertine has a unique look but needs sealing and careful cleaning. Wood-effect porcelain planks give a timber look without the maintenance. Patterned and encaustic-style tiles work well in hallways and small spaces. For kitchens and hallways, look for a slip rating and consider a mid-tone grout, which hides everyday dirt better than white. Keep a few spare tiles from the same batch, as shades and sizes can vary between production runs.

Underfloor heating, movement joints and doorways

Tile is one of the best floor coverings for underfloor heating because it conducts heat well. Electric mats can be laid on an insulation board and set in self-levelling compound before tiling; the final connection must be made by a qualified electrician. Large floors and floors crossing between rooms need movement joints, flexible gaps filled with silicone or a movement profile, so that expansion doesn't crack tiles. At doorways, we plan tile heights with thresholds and door clearances in mind, and doors may need trimming. We'll also plan where tile lines meet the kitchen units and any existing flooring.

How long it takes

Typically 2–3 days for a kitchen floor, plus drying time for any levelling

Questions about floor tiling

Can you tile over floorboards?

Not directly. Floorboards flex and move, which will crack tiles and grout. The usual approach is to screw exterior-grade plywood or tile backer boards over the boards to stiffen the floor, and sometimes to add a decoupling membrane. We'll check that the joists and boards are sound before we start.

How long before I can walk on a new tiled floor?

Usually after around 24 hours with standard adhesive, although rapid-set adhesives can allow foot traffic within a few hours. It's best to avoid heavy furniture and appliances for a couple of days, until the adhesive and grout have fully cured. We'll let you know what products we've used and how long to wait.

Which tiles are best for a kitchen floor?

Porcelain is usually the best choice, as it's very hard, resists stains and doesn't need sealing. Choose a tile with a decent slip rating and a mid-tone grout that won't show dirt. Very large tiles need a very flat floor, so the size you choose may affect how much preparation is needed.
