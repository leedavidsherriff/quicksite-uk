A smooth, fresh surface over tired walls

A thin finishing coat that turns cracked, patchy walls into a smooth base for paint.

Skimming is the most common plastering job in UK homes. It puts a thin coat of finishing plaster, usually two passes to a total of around two to three millimetres, over existing sound walls and ceilings. It hides hairline cracks, patched repairs, old wallpaper damage and uneven textures, leaving a smooth surface ready to paint. If you're in Devizes and your walls look rough under new paint, or you've stripped paper and found a patchwork, a skim is usually the answer. We protect the room, prepare the surface properly and leave it clean, with drying advice before you decorate.

When a skim works and when it won't

A skim only works over a surface that is solid. We tap walls to find hollow or blown patches, which sound dull and drummy, and these are cut out and patched first. Loose paint and wallpaper are stripped. Cracks are checked, because a crack that keeps opening points to movement, and skimming over it will simply crack again. If most of the wall is hollow or crumbling, especially in older houses with original lime or gypsum backing, a skim is false economy and replastering is the right job. We'll tell you which one you need after looking, not before.

Preparing the surface

Most painted walls are sealed with a bonding agent, usually a diluted PVA or a gritted primer, so the fresh plaster grips and doesn't dry too quickly. Where there are deep dents or old repairs, a bonding coat or fast-setting patching plaster brings them up to level. External corners get new metal or plastic angle beads for sharp, knock-resistant edges, and scrim tape is bedded over cracks and board joints. Sockets and switch plates are loosened by a competent person so we can plaster behind them. Floors are sheeted and the room is cleared or furniture wrapped. Where walls have been chased for cables, the chases are filled flush a day ahead so they don't sink under the skim.

Drying out and decorating

Fresh skim goes from dark pink to an even pale pink as it dries, and patchy darker areas mean it's still wet. In a heated, ventilated room most skims are dry in three to seven days, but cold or damp conditions can stretch that to two weeks. Don't speed it up with blasting heaters, which can cause cracking. The first coat of paint must be a mist coat, a watered-down contract matt emulsion that soaks in and seals the plaster. Vinyl or wipeable paints applied straight onto new plaster tend to peel, because they can't soak in.

How long it takes

Typically 1 day per room; 3–7 days drying before decorating

Questions about skimming walls and ceilings

How long after skimming can I paint?

Usually once the plaster has turned an even light pink with no dark patches, which takes about three to seven days in a warm, ventilated room. In winter or in a damp room it can take two weeks. Apply a mist coat of watered-down matt emulsion first, then your normal coats. Painting too early traps moisture and causes peeling.

Can you skim over artex or woodchip?

Woodchip must be stripped first, as it will bubble under wet plaster. Artex can often be skimmed over once sealed with a bonding agent, and a heavily textured pattern may need a bonding coat first. Artex applied before the mid-1980s may contain asbestos, so we'll recommend a test before any sanding or scraping takes place.

Why has my new plaster cracked?

Fine cracks usually come from drying too quickly, often because heaters were used or the room was very warm. Cracks along board joints mean they weren't taped, and longer cracks can come from movement in the building. Small shrinkage cracks can be filled when you decorate. Anything that reopens should be looked at before it's replastered.
