Patios laid on a proper base

Porcelain, sandstone or flags on a compacted base and full mortar bed.

Get my guide priceCall 07912 690100

A patio is the part of a garden you use most, and the part that shows poor workmanship fastest. Rocking slabs, puddles by the back door and weeds sprouting from every joint almost always come from the groundwork, not the stone. We dig out properly, build a compacted base, lay every slab on a full bed of mortar and set falls that carry rainwater away from your walls. Then we help you choose between porcelain, natural sandstone and concrete flags, based on how you will use the space and how much upkeep you want.

What goes underneath

For a patio used for walking and furniture, we dig out to around 150–200 mm, lay and compact roughly 100 mm of MOT Type 1 crushed stone, then bed each slab on a full, solid mortar bed. Laying slabs on five dabs of mortar, known as dot-and-dab, leaves hollows that crack slabs, trap water and invite ants. The finished surface should sit at least 150 mm below the damp-proof course of your house, and fall away from it at around 1 in 60, so water drains to the lawn, a border or a channel drain rather than towards the back door.

Porcelain, sandstone or flags

Outdoor porcelain, usually 20 mm thick, is dense, stain-resistant and consistent in colour, and hardly grows algae; it needs a primer on the back of each slab to bond to the mortar. Indian sandstone has natural colour variation and texture, is slightly easier to cut to curves, and darkens when wet. It is porous, so it can pick up green algae and occasional iron staining, and some people seal it. Calibrated sandstone is cut to an even thickness and lays more precisely than hand-cut riven stone. Concrete flags are the budget option and are hard-wearing, but they fade and look less refined.

Joints that stay weed-free

Joints are where patios usually let you down. Traditional sand and cement pointing looks crisp but can crack over time, and on porcelain it must be done carefully to avoid staining the surface. Brush-in jointing compounds, which are resin-based and set hard, are popular because they are quick and neat and resist weeds well, although they need the right weather to cure. On porcelain, narrow 3–5 mm joints look sharpest; natural stone suits 10 mm or so. Whatever the method, joints should be filled to the full depth, not just skimmed on top.

Planning, steps and levels

A ground-level patio in a back garden normally needs no planning permission. The front-garden rule, where more than 5 m² of impermeable surface needs permission unless it drains within the property, applies to front gardens only. If the garden slopes, we can build a level terrace with a low retaining wall and steps; steps work best with a consistent rise of around 150 mm and a tread of at least 300 mm. Raised patios and platforms more than 30 cm above the ground usually need planning permission, so we check with you first.

Questions about patios

How much does a new patio cost?

As a guide, a fully installed patio runs from around £70 per m² for concrete flags, around £95 for calibrated Indian sandstone and around £120 for 20 mm porcelain. Groundwork, steps, walls and drainage are the main things that change the final price.

Is porcelain better than Indian sandstone?

Porcelain is more stain-resistant, grows less algae and stays consistent in colour, which suits modern gardens. Sandstone has natural character, is easier to cut and softens with age, which suits traditional homes. Porcelain costs a little more and needs more care to lay properly.

Why are my patio slabs wobbling?

Rocking slabs almost always mean they were laid on dots of mortar or a thin sand bed rather than a full mortar bed, or the base was never properly compacted. The fix is to lift the affected slabs, clean them, and relay them on a full bed.
