Artificial grass that drains and stays flat

A free-draining compacted base, weed membrane and tidy fixed edges.

Get my guide priceCall 07912 690100

Artificial grass is a good answer for shady gardens where real grass struggles, busy families, dog owners and anyone who never wants to mow again. The difference between a lawn that looks convincing for years and one that ripples, smells and sprouts weeds is the base underneath. We dig out, build a compacted free-draining sub-base, lay a weed membrane, fix solid edges and join the rolls so the seams disappear. We also give you honest advice on its downsides, including heat in full sun and what it means for garden wildlife.

The base makes the lawn

We remove the existing turf and dig out around 75–100 mm, then lay and compact a sub-base of MOT Type 1 or crushed stone, topped with 20–25 mm of granite dust or fine grit as a smooth laying course. A weed membrane goes under or over this layer. The base must drain freely, because the grass is perforated and water passes straight through; a solid, badly draining base leaves puddles and smells. Timber, composite or steel edging is fixed around the perimeter and the grass is secured to it. Laying straight onto soil or old turf leads to dips and weeds within months.

Choosing a grass

Pile height of around 30–40 mm looks most natural in a garden; shorter piles are more hard-wearing and easier to clean. Look for a mix of blade colours with a brown thatch layer, which makes it look like real grass, and a UV-stabilised product that will not fade quickly. Heavier face weights generally feel denser and last longer. For pets, choose a grass with good drainage and consider an antibacterial infill. Direction matters too: the pile has a grain, so the rolls should all face the same way, ideally towards the house, for the most natural look.

Joins, infill and finishing

Where two rolls meet, the edges are trimmed along the stitch lines and bonded onto joining tape with outdoor adhesive, so the seam is invisible from the house. Kiln-dried sand is then brushed in as an infill; it weighs the grass down, helps the blades stand upright and protects the backing from UV. Some products need no infill. On existing concrete or paving, a foam underlay can be used instead of a dug-out base, as long as the surface drains. The whole area is power-brushed at the end to lift the pile.

The honest downsides

Artificial grass gets hot in strong, direct sun, and dark-coloured or dense products can be uncomfortable barefoot on south-facing lawns in summer. Mirrors and glass that reflect sunlight onto it can melt the fibres. It needs occasional brushing, leaf clearing and, with pets, regular rinsing. It is a plastic product that offers little for insects and birds and has to be disposed of eventually, so many people keep planting borders around it. If you have a sunny, open garden and do not mind mowing, real turf may still suit you better.

How long it takes

Typically 2–3 days for a 40 m² lawn

Questions about artificial grass

Is artificial grass good for dogs?

It can be, as long as the base drains well and you rinse it regularly. Solids are picked up as on real grass, and urine drains through the backing. An antibacterial infill or an enzyme cleaner helps control smells. Very determined diggers can damage edges, so fixing matters.

How long does artificial grass last?

A good-quality, UV-stabilised grass on a proper base typically lasts ten to fifteen years in a domestic garden. Heavy use, pets, strong sun and poor maintenance shorten it. Regular brushing keeps the pile upright and stops it looking flat in walkways. Keep barbecues and reflective glass away from it.

Will weeds grow through artificial grass?

Not through a properly laid lawn with a weed membrane and a compacted base. Occasional weeds can root in dirt and seeds that land on top or at the edges, but they are shallow and easy to pull out. A brush and an occasional weed treatment keeps it clear.
