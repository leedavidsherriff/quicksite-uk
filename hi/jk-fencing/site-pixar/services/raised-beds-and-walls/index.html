Raised beds and walls that stay straight

Sleeper beds, brick walls and retaining walls on proper footings with drainage.

Get my guide priceCall 07912 690100

Raised beds and low walls give a garden structure, split it into spaces and make planting easier on the back. On sloping plots, retaining walls turn an awkward bank into level terraces you can actually use. They also take real loads: wet soil is very heavy, and a wall or bed that is not built for it will bow, lean or crack within a few years. We build on proper footings, with drainage behind anything holding back soil, using sleepers, brick, block or stone to suit the look of your house and garden.

Sleeper raised beds

Timber sleepers are the quickest way to build a raised bed with a natural look. We use new pressure-treated softwood or oak sleepers, stacked in staggered courses and fixed with long timber screws or steel pins, with corner posts for taller beds. Old reclaimed railway sleepers often contain creosote or tar, which can leach into soil and is not suitable where you grow food. A permeable membrane on the inside faces slows rot, and an open base lets water drain away. Beds around 450–600 mm high are comfortable to work at and give plenty of root depth.

Brick and block garden walls

A freestanding garden wall needs a concrete strip footing, typically at least 150 mm deep and twice the width of the wall, set below frost level on firm ground. Walls one brick thick are much stronger than half-brick walls, which should only be used for low planters and must be supported with piers. Frost-resistant bricks and a proper coping or brick-on-edge on top stop water soaking in and freezing. Rendered block walls are a cost-effective modern alternative. Under permitted development, garden walls are generally limited to 1 m next to a road or 2 m elsewhere.

Retaining walls

Retaining walls hold back soil on a slope, and the pressure behind them rises sharply with height, especially when the soil is wet. For low retaining walls up to around 600–700 mm, a well-built block, brick or sleeper wall with a solid footing is usually fine. Behind every retaining wall we lay free-draining gravel and a perforated pipe, and leave weep holes near the base, so water can escape instead of pushing the wall over. Retaining walls over about 1 m, or near buildings, driveways or boundaries, should be designed by a structural engineer.

Filling and planting

Raised beds need the right filling to be useful. A layer of rubble or coarse material at the base of deep beds helps drainage, topped with a good blend of topsoil and compost. Beds for vegetables benefit from a richer mix, while beds for Mediterranean plants and herbs prefer something grittier. Soil settles over the first year, so expect to top it up. We can supply and fill beds, and plant them with you or leave them ready for your own planting. Wall capping stones can double as seating around the edge of a patio.

Questions about raised beds and garden walls

Are railway sleepers safe for vegetable beds?

Old reclaimed railway sleepers often contain creosote or tar, which can leach into the soil, so they are not suitable for growing food. New pressure-treated softwood or oak sleepers are the safe choice. Lining the inside with a membrane also slows rot and keeps soil off the timber.

Do I need planning permission for a garden wall?

Usually not, if it is no more than 1 m high next to a road or footpath used by vehicles, or 2 m high elsewhere. Listed buildings and conservation areas have extra rules. Retaining walls holding back significant soil near a boundary may need engineer design, even without planning permission.

Why is my garden wall leaning?

Leaning walls usually have a shallow or missing footing, no drainage behind them, or are holding back more soil than they were built for. Tree roots can also push walls over. A leaning retaining wall should be checked quickly, because it can fail suddenly after heavy rain.
