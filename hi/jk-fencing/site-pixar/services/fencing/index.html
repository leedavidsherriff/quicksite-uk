Fences that stay standing through winter gales

Panels, close-board, slatted or composite, set deep on solid posts.

Get my guide priceCall 07912 690100

A fence has to stand up to every gale for years, so what holds it up matters more than the panels themselves. Most failed fences come down because posts were set too shallow, rotted at ground level or were never properly concreted in. We set posts deep, use gravel boards so timber never touches the soil, and fix panels or boards so they cannot rattle loose. We can replace a single blown panel, renew a whole boundary or build a smart slatted screen, and we will talk you through what suits your garden and budget.

Posts: concrete or timber

Concrete posts with concrete gravel boards are the longest-lasting option and cannot rot; panels simply slot into their grooves, so a blown or damaged panel can be swapped in minutes. Timber posts look warmer and suit close-board and slatted designs, but they must be pressure-treated to UC4, which is rated for ground contact, and around 100 × 100 mm for a 1.8 m fence. Either way, posts should go about 600 mm into the ground for a standard 1.8 m fence, set in concrete or postcrete, with the top of the concrete sloped to shed water away from the post.

Choosing a fence style

Overlap or lap panels are the most affordable, but they are the most likely to blow out in exposed spots. Close-board fencing, with vertical featheredge boards nailed to horizontal rails, is much stronger and gives better privacy, and it can follow a slope neatly. Slatted or Venetian fencing, with horizontal slats and small gaps, looks modern and lets wind pass through, which reduces the load on the posts. Composite fencing costs more but never needs painting and will not rot or warp. On windy, open sites, a fence that filters the wind usually lasts longer than a solid one.

Height, boundaries and neighbours

Under permitted development rules you can usually put up a fence up to 2 m high without planning permission, or 1 m high where it is next to a road or footpath used by vehicles. Height is measured from the ground on your side, and trellis on top counts towards it. Listed buildings, conservation areas and some new estates have extra rules. Before replacing a boundary fence, check who owns it on your deeds; T marks on the plan often show responsibility. Talking to neighbours first saves disputes, particularly about which side the good face goes.

Making a fence last

Gravel boards along the bottom keep the panels off the soil, stopping rot where most fences fail first. Tops of timber posts benefit from caps to keep rain out of the end grain. Pressure-treated timber can be left to weather to a silver grey or painted with a water-based fence stain every few years; avoid heavy climbing plants pulling on panels, and keep soil and compost bins away from the boards. A well-built timber fence on concrete posts should last fifteen to twenty years or more, and the posts much longer.

Questions about fencing

How high can my garden fence be?

In most gardens you can put up a fence up to 2 m high without planning permission, or 1 m next to a road or footpath used by vehicles. Trellis counts towards the height. Listed buildings, conservation areas and some estates have extra restrictions.

Which side of the fence faces my neighbour?

There is no law requiring the good side to face out, but it is common courtesy and many deeds or estate covenants expect it. On a boundary you own, the posts and rails usually sit on your side. It is always worth agreeing it with your neighbour first.

Why do fence panels blow down?

Usually because posts were set too shallow, have rotted at ground level, or the panels were a thin lap design on an exposed site. Concrete posts, deeper footings and slatted or close-board designs that handle wind better are the long-term fix.
