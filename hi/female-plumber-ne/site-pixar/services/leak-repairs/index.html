Leaks found, fixed and dried out

Fast repairs for burst pipes, dripping joints and leaks you can hear but can't see.

Water always finds the lowest point, so the stain on your ceiling is rarely directly below the leak. Most household leaks come from a handful of places: a weeping compression joint under a sink, a failed toilet seal, perished flexible hoses, a shower tray that has dropped, or a pipe that froze and split. We trace the source first, then repair it properly rather than wrapping it in tape. This page explains what to do in the first ten minutes, how leaks are found, and what a typical repair costs, so you can make a calm decision even when water is coming through the light fitting.

The first ten minutes

Turn off the water at the main stopcock, usually under the kitchen sink, in a downstairs cupboard or where the supply enters the house. If water is near light fittings or sockets, switch off the electricity at the consumer unit and do not touch wet switches. Open the cold taps downstairs to drain the pipes, and if you have a hot water cylinder, turn off the boiler or immersion. Put a bucket under a bulging ceiling and, if it is safe, pierce the bulge with a screwdriver so the water drains in one place rather than bringing the ceiling down.

How we find a hidden leak

We start with the simple checks: watch the water meter with every tap off, look for tide marks, and feel along accessible pipe runs. Pressure-testing individual sections tells us whether the leak is on the hot or cold side, or the heating circuit. For leaks under floors or in walls we use a moisture meter and a thermal camera to follow the damp without ripping everything out. Where we do need to open up, we cut a neat, repairable hole rather than chasing plaster, and tell you before we make it.

Repair, not a patch

A slip coupling or repair clamp can stop a burst pipe quickly, but it is a temporary measure. Our aim is a permanent repair: cut out the damaged section, replace it with new copper or barrier plastic pipe, and use soldered, press-fit or push-fit joints suited to the location. Joints hidden under floors should be soldered or press-fit where possible, since they can be very hard to reach later. If the pipe is corroded along its length, we will tell you so you can decide whether to replace more of it now rather than face another leak.

Insurance and drying out

Many home insurance policies cover the damage caused by an escape of water, and some cover the cost of tracing the leak too. Take photos before anything is moved, keep the damaged pipe section if possible, and ask us for a written report of what failed and why, which insurers usually want. After the repair, ceilings and floors need time to dry before they are decorated. A moisture reading tells you when it is safe to paint, which is often longer than people expect.

Questions about leaks & burst pipes

Where is my stopcock?

Most homes have an internal stopcock where the mains enters the house, often under the kitchen sink, in a downstairs toilet, under the stairs or in a garage. There is also an external stop tap under a small cover near the boundary, which belongs to the water company. Find yours now and check it turns, because stiff stopcocks often seize just when you need them.

Is a leak covered by my home insurance?

Many buildings and contents policies cover damage from an escape of water, and some include trace and access cover to find the leak. The repair of the pipe itself is often excluded. Check your policy wording, take photos before you tidy up, and ask us for a written report explaining what failed. Insurers generally process claims faster with that evidence.

How do I know if I have a hidden leak?

Signs include the boiler pressure dropping repeatedly, a water meter that moves with every tap off, damp patches or musty smells, lifting floor tiles, the sound of running water at night, and higher water bills. A quick meter test is a good start: note the reading, use no water for an hour, then check again.
