A dripping tap or a toilet that never quite stops running is easy to ignore, but they waste a surprising amount of water and, on a meter, money. A constantly running cistern can waste hundreds of litres a day. These are also the jobs many firms do not want, which is why they get left. We are happy to take them on. Most repairs are done in one visit with parts carried on the van, and where a replacement makes more sense than a repair, we will tell you why and give you the price for both.

Dripping and stiff taps

Older taps with rubber washers drip when the washer wears, and a new washer and a reseat of the valve is often all they need. Most modern taps use ceramic disc cartridges instead, which either seal or fail, and are usually fixed by fitting a matching cartridge. A dripping mixer can also be a worn O-ring at the spout. Hard water shortens the life of all of these. If your tap is a cheap unbranded one with no spare parts available, replacing it with a branded tap is often better value than trying to repair it.

Running toilets and weak flushes

If water trickles into the pan long after flushing, the flush valve seal has usually worn or the fill valve is set too high and water is escaping down the overflow. Both are cheap parts that can be replaced in an hour. A weak flush can be a partly blocked pan, a flush valve that is not lifting fully, or a cistern filling too slowly because of a failing fill valve. A toilet that rocks needs attention, because movement breaks the seal on the pan connector and can leak unseen under the floor.

Replacing a toilet or basin

Replacing a toilet is straightforward if the new pan lines up with the existing soil pipe. Close-coupled toilets vary in how far the outlet sits from the wall, so we check the measurements before you buy. Back-to-wall and wall-hung pans need concealed cisterns and frames, which is a larger job. For basins, check the tap hole count and whether you want a pedestal, a semi-pedestal or a vanity unit that gives you storage and hides the pipework. We fit new isolation valves and wastes as part of the job.

Why isolation valves matter

An isolation valve is a small valve on the pipe feeding each tap and toilet, turned with a flat screwdriver. With one fitted, you can shut off a single appliance without turning off the whole house. Many older homes have none, which turns every small repair into draining the system. We fit them as standard, and it is worth knowing where they are, because they are the quickest way to stop a leak from a toilet or tap. If yours are stiff, a quarter turn now and then keeps them free.

Questions about taps, toilets & sinks

How much water does a running toilet waste?

A cistern that constantly trickles into the pan can waste anything from around 200 to 400 litres a day, depending on how bad the leak is. On a water meter that adds up quickly. A simple test is to put a few drops of food colouring in the cistern and see whether colour appears in the pan without flushing.

Should I repair or replace my tap?

If the tap is from a known brand and the body is sound, a new cartridge or washer is usually the cheaper and quicker fix. If parts are unavailable, the chrome is flaking, or it has been repaired before, replacement is often better value. We will give you the price for both options so you can choose.

Why does my toilet keep running after I flush?

The two usual causes are a worn seal on the flush valve, which lets water leak straight into the pan, or a fill valve set too high so water escapes down the overflow. Both are inexpensive parts. Hard water and debris can also stop the fill valve closing properly.
