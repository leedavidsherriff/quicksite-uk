Wood floors laid flat, quiet and built to last

Engineered wood, laminate, solid oak and herringbone fitted onto sound, level bases.

A wood floor can transform a room, but a poorly laid one squeaks, gaps, lifts at the edges or flexes underfoot. Most of those problems come from what's underneath, not the boards themselves. We fit engineered wood, solid wood, laminate and herringbone parquet floors in homes across Pontypridd, onto concrete, screed and timber subfloors. We'll check levels and moisture, recommend the right underlay, and finish with scotia, skirting or neat thresholds. Doors are trimmed so they clear the new floor, and every room is left clean with the offcuts taken away.

Engineered, solid or laminate

Engineered wood has a real hardwood top layer bonded to a stable plywood or softwood core. It looks and feels like solid wood but moves far less, making it suitable over underfloor heating and on concrete. Thicker wear layers can be sanded and refinished several times. Solid wood is traditional and can be sanded many times, but it expands and contracts more with changes in humidity and is less suited to kitchens and heated floors. Laminate is a photographic image on a fibreboard core; it's hard-wearing and affordable, but can't be sanded. Luxury vinyl tile, or LVT, is waterproof and good for kitchens and bathrooms.

Preparing the subfloor

Before fitting, the subfloor must be flat, dry and sound. Concrete and screed floors are tested for moisture, and a damp-proof membrane or moisture-barrier underlay is laid if needed. Uneven floors are levelled with a self-levelling compound or by sanding high spots. Timber floors are checked for loose or squeaky boards, which are screwed down, and may be overlaid with ply. Wood flooring should be left in the room for a couple of days, still in its packs, to acclimatise to the temperature and humidity before fitting.

Floating, glued or nailed

Most laminate and many engineered floors are laid floating, with boards clicked together over an underlay and not fixed to the subfloor. It's quick and allows the floor to move, but needs a good acoustic underlay to avoid a hollow sound. Glued-down floors feel solid and quiet underfoot and are recommended over underfloor heating by many manufacturers. Secret-nailing solid wood to timber joists or battens is traditional. In every case, an expansion gap of around 10 to 15 mm is left around the edges, hidden under skirting or scotia, so the floor can move without buckling.

Herringbone and parquet

Herringbone and chevron patterns have become very popular, and they create a striking, classic look in hallways, living rooms and kitchens. They take more time, skill and material than straight boards, because each block is laid individually and the pattern must be set out from a centre line so it runs straight through the room. Borders around the edges frame the pattern. Most herringbone floors are glued down onto a very flat subfloor. They're priced higher per square metre but add character that a plank floor can't.

How long it takes

Typically 1 day for an average living room; 2–3 days for herringbone

Questions about wood and laminate flooring

Can wood flooring go over underfloor heating?

Engineered wood is usually the best choice over underfloor heating, as it's more stable than solid wood. Check the manufacturer's guidance for the maximum surface temperature and approved fitting methods, which often includes gluing down. Laminate and LVT can also work, as long as they are rated for underfloor heating.

Should I remove skirting boards for new flooring?

For the neatest finish, yes. Removing skirting and refitting it over the floor hides the expansion gap without scotia beading. It's more work, and can damage old plaster, so on many jobs a neat scotia or quadrant bead is fitted instead. We'll talk through both options.

How long does engineered wood need to acclimatise?

Most manufacturers recommend leaving the packs flat in the room for at least 48 hours before fitting, at normal living temperature. Solid wood may need longer. This allows the boards to adjust to the room's humidity, reducing the chance of gaps or cupping later.
