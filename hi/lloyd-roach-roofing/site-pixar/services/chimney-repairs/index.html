Chimney repairs, done while the scaffold is up

Repointing, flaunching, lead flashings, caps and stack removal.

Get my guide priceCall 07811 339098

Chimney stacks take the worst of the weather, and because they are hard to see from the ground, problems go unnoticed until damp shows on a chimney breast or a pot comes loose in a gale. Most chimney leaks come from worn pointing, cracked flaunching around the pots or failed lead flashings where the stack meets the roof. We repair all of these, cap chimneys that are no longer used, and can take down a stack you do not need. Because nearly every chimney job needs scaffold, it pays to combine it with any other roof work.

Pointing, flaunching and pots

Mortar joints on a stack weather much faster than those on a wall. Once they are eroded, rain soaks into the brickwork and through to the chimney breast inside. Repointing means raking out the old joints to a good depth and refilling with a mortar suited to your brick, usually lime-based on older houses. The flaunching is the sloped mortar cap around the pots, designed to shed water; when it cracks, water gets straight down the inside of the stack. We rebuild it and bed loose pots securely. Leaning or badly cracked stacks may need partial rebuilding.

Lead flashings

Where a chimney meets the roof slope, lead flashings stop water running into the gap. A proper installation uses step flashings and soakers down the sides, a front apron and a back gutter behind the stack, typically in Code 4 lead, with the tops dressed into cut mortar joints. Common failures are lead that has been bedded only in mastic or mortar fillets, splits from thermal movement where pieces are too long, and flashings stolen for scrap. Lead substitutes exist and are sometimes used where theft is a risk, but traditional lead lasts longest when it is fitted correctly.

Capping or removing a chimney

If you no longer use an open fire, a chimney can be capped to keep rain and birds out. It must still be ventilated, usually with a vented cap on top and an air vent in the chimney breast inside, otherwise condensation will cause damp in the flue. A stack can also be removed down to below the roofline, with the roof then tiled or slated over. Removing a stack changes the structure, so if the chimney breast inside is removed too, building regulations apply and the remaining brickwork must be properly supported. Shared stacks on semis and terraces fall under the Party Wall Act.

Why chimney work nearly always needs scaffold

A chimney stack sits at the highest, most exposed point of the roof, often with a steep slope below it, so it cannot be worked on safely from a ladder. A proper chimney scaffold, or a full scaffold with a platform around the stack, lets us rake out joints, lift and rebed pots and dress lead correctly rather than rushing. It is often the largest single cost on a chimney job, which is why we show it separately on the quote. If you are also thinking about roof repairs, a dry ridge or new gutters, doing them while the scaffold is up saves paying for access twice.

Questions about chimney repairs

Why is my chimney breast damp?

The most common causes are worn pointing on the stack, cracked flaunching, failed lead flashings, or an uncapped chimney letting rain straight in. A capped chimney with no ventilation can also suffer condensation. Damp can take weeks to dry out after the repair, so staining may linger for a while.

Do I need permission to remove a chimney stack?

Removing a stack is usually allowed without planning permission, unless the house is listed or in a conservation area. If you also remove the chimney breast inside, building regulations apply and support is required. On a shared stack, you must serve notice on your neighbour under the Party Wall Act.

Can I just cap my chimney?

Yes, but it must stay ventilated. A vented cap at the top and a vent in the chimney breast lets air move through the flue and stops condensation causing damp. Fully sealing both ends is a common mistake that creates damp patches inside.
