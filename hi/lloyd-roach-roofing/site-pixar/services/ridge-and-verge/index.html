Ridge and verge repairs that stay put in a storm

Rebed or upgrade to mechanically fixed dry ridge and dry verge systems.

Get my guide priceCall 07811 339098

Ridge tiles along the top of the roof and the verges along its gable ends are the parts most exposed to wind. On older roofs they are held on only by sand and cement, which cracks and washes out over time until tiles rock loose and blow off in the next storm. You may see mortar fragments in the gutters or on the drive, or a gap along the edge of the roof. We rebed them properly, or replace the mortar with a dry-fix system that screws each tile down and needs no repointing again.

Why mortar alone is no longer enough

Since the 2014 revision of BS 5534, the British Standard for slating and tiling, ridge, hip and verge tiles on new and replacement roofs must be mechanically fixed, not held by mortar alone. The reason is simple: mortar shrinks, cracks and loses its grip, especially on a moving timber roof, and storm damage claims were dominated by lifted ridges and verges. Mortar can still be used as a bedding in a traditional repair, but each ridge tile should also be fixed with a screw, clip or strap to a ridge batten or bracket beneath.

Dry ridge and dry verge systems

A dry ridge system uses a roll-out ventilated strip under the ridge tiles, with each tile screwed down to a batten on adjustable brackets. It ventilates the roof along its apex, which helps prevent loft condensation, and needs no repointing. A dry verge system uses interlocking plastic caps that clip over the ends of the tiles at the gable, closing the gap and fixing the tiles in place. Both are quick to fit on concrete tile roofs, and there are versions for slate and clay. Colours are matched to your tiles as closely as possible.

When a traditional repair is better

On older houses, conservation areas and natural slate roofs with clay or stone ridges, a traditional mortar finish is often the look you want to keep. Here we lift the ridge tiles, clean them, fit a ridge batten, and rebed each tile in a suitable mortar while mechanically fixing it to the batten. On slate verges, a neat mortar undercloak finish can be renewed using a stronger mix and fixings. Lime mortars are sometimes preferred for older buildings because they flex with movement. We will discuss what suits your house rather than fit plastic everywhere.

Signs your ridge or verges need attention

From the ground, look along the top of the roof for tiles that sit unevenly or have visible gaps beneath them, and along the gable edges for missing or cracked mortar. Mortar crumbs in the gutter or on the patio are an early warning. Inside the loft, daylight along the apex or at the gable wall shows where the bedding has failed. Ridge tiles are heavy, so a loose one falling from two storeys is a genuine safety risk as well as a leak. Catching the problem early usually means a simple rebed or dry-fix upgrade rather than repairs to storm damage.

Questions about ridge and verge repairs

Is a dry ridge better than mortar?

For most concrete tile roofs, yes. A dry ridge is mechanically fixed, ventilates the loft and never needs repointing. Mortar gives a traditional look that some prefer on older houses, but it must be combined with mechanical fixings to meet the current British Standard.

Why is there mortar in my gutters?

Mortar pieces in gutters or on the ground usually come from crumbling ridge or verge bedding. As it washes out, tiles lose their support and can rock loose or blow off in strong winds. It is worth having the ridge and verges checked before the next stormy season.

Do dry verge caps look plastic?

They are plastic, but modern caps are slim, colour-matched and neat. On a typical modern concrete-tile house they blend in well from the ground. On older houses or in conservation areas, a rebedded mortar verge may look more appropriate and is sometimes required.
