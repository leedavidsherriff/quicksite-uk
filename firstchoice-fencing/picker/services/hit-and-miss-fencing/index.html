Hit and miss fencing: the same from both sides

Boards alternate either side of the rails, so both gardens get the good side and the wind gets through.

See guide prices WhatsAppCall 07305 790488

Hit and miss fencing, sometimes called double-sided or neighbour-friendly fencing, has vertical boards fixed alternately on one side of the rails and then the other. Look at it straight on and it is solid; look from an angle and you see through the gaps. It has two big advantages. Both gardens get the same, finished face, which ends the argument about who gets the good side, and air passes through the gaps, so the fence takes far less load in a gale than a solid screen. That makes it a sensible choice for exposed, open gardens where solid fences keep blowing over.

How it is built

Posts are set in concrete as with any fence, with three rails between them for a 1.8 metre fence. Boards, usually around 100 to 150 mm wide, are fixed to one side of the rails with a gap slightly narrower than a board, and the next set of boards is fixed to the other side to cover those gaps with a small overlap. We use planed or sawn boards depending on the look you want, and we fix them with coated nails or screws. A capping rail and gravel board finish it the same way as closeboard, keeping water off the end grain and the boards clear of the soil.

How private is it

From straight on, hit and miss is solid. Stand close and look along it at an angle and you will see through the gaps, which is the trade-off for letting the air through. If full privacy matters on part of the boundary, beside a patio for example, we can widen the overlap there or switch to closeboard for that section and keep hit and miss for the rest, so the fence looks consistent while doing different jobs along its length.

Why it copes with wind

A solid 1.8 metre fence acts like a sail. In a strong gust the whole load goes into the posts, and that is why so many fences in open estates lean or snap at ground level after a winter. Hit and miss lets a good share of that air pass through, so the load on each post is lower and the fence stays straighter for longer. It still needs proper footings: posts about 600 mm deep in concrete, and concrete posts where the garden is very exposed.

Finish and upkeep

Because both faces are the good face, hit and miss is a natural choice if you want to stain the fence, as both you and your neighbour see the colour. Treated timber weathers to grey if left alone. A water-based stain every two or three years keeps the colour, and the gaps make it quicker to reach both sides. Keep climbers trained on wires or trellis rather than woven through the boards, as they hold moisture against the timber.

How long it takes

Typically 2 days for 20 to 25 metres, weather allowing.

Questions

Hit and miss fencing: what people ask.

Is hit and miss fencing good for windy gardens?

It is one of the best timber choices for an exposed garden, because air passes through the gaps and the posts take less load in a gale. It still needs good footings, and concrete posts are worth it in very open spots.

Can you see through hit and miss fencing?

Not from straight on. At an angle and up close you can see through the gaps. If one section needs to be fully private we can widen the overlap there or use closeboard for that stretch.

Does hit and miss cost more than closeboard?

Usually a little, because it uses more boards and more fixing time per metre. As a guide, 1.8 m hit and miss runs about £88 to £100 per metre fitted, against about £68 to £78 for closeboard on concrete posts.
