Closeboard fencing, built board by board

Feather-edge boards on rails, concrete posts and gravel boards: the hard-wearing garden standard.

See guide prices WhatsAppCall 07305 790488

Closeboard is the fence most Scottish back gardens should have and many don't. Instead of thin woven or lap panels that rattle loose in the first winter gale, it is built in place: posts set in concrete, two or three rails between them, and tapered feather-edge boards nailed on one at a time so each one overlaps the last. The result is a solid, private screen with no gaps, strong enough for an exposed garden and easy to repair one board at a time if something hits it. It is also the most economical proper fence per metre, which is why it suits long boundaries on semis and ex-council gardens.

How a closeboard fence goes together

Posts go in first, usually every 2.4 to 3 metres for timber and 1.83 metres for slotted concrete, set about 600 mm into the ground on a concrete footing. Rails, called arris or cant rails, run between them, and the feather-edge boards are fixed vertically to the rails with an overlap of around 25 mm so that as the timber shrinks and swells there is never a gap to see through. At the foot sits a gravel board, a sacrificial board in concrete or treated timber, so the fence boards never touch wet soil. A capping rail along the top throws rain off the end grain of the boards.

Concrete posts or timber posts

Concrete posts cost a little more but they do not rot, and in a windy Lanarkshire garden that matters: the fence usually fails at the post, just at ground level, where timber stays wet. Concrete posts with concrete gravel boards give a fence that can outlast two sets of boards. Timber posts look warmer and suit a garden where the fence is the feature; if we use them, they are pressure-treated to the grade meant for ground contact, and we set them in concrete with the top of the footing shaped so water runs away from the post.

Height, the good side and your neighbour

Most back-garden closeboard goes up at 1.8 metres, which gives privacy without the fence towering over the garden. Around 1.2 metres is common at the front. The boards go on one side of the rails, so one face is smooth and the other shows the rails and posts; there is no legal rule about which way it faces, but most people put the smooth side towards the neighbour or the street, and it is worth agreeing that with next door before we start. In Scotland, who owns and maintains a boundary fence is set by the title deeds, and many are shared, so check yours if it is a replacement.

Looking after it

Pressure-treated timber is protected against rot but it still weathers to silver grey within a year or two. If you want it to keep a colour, a water-based fence stain every two or three years does it, and we can apply the first coat when the timber has had a few weeks to dry out. Keep soil, bark and climbing plants off the gravel boards, and clear leaves from the base in autumn. If a board gets split by a ball or a branch, it can be replaced on its own in minutes, which is one of the main reasons to choose closeboard over panels.

How long it takes

Typically 1 to 2 days for 20 to 25 metres, weather allowing.

Questions

Closeboard fencing: what people ask.

Is closeboard better than fence panels?

For most gardens, yes. Lap and woven panels are thin and slot between posts, so they rattle and blow out in high winds. Closeboard is built in place with boards fixed to rails, so it is stiffer, has no gaps and can be repaired one board at a time. It costs a little more per metre than cheap panels but usually lasts far longer.

How long does a closeboard fence last?

With pressure-treated boards, gravel boards and good post footings, 15 to 20 years is a fair expectation, and concrete posts can outlast the boards. The biggest things that shorten its life are soil or plants against the timber and posts set too shallow for the wind.

What is the difference between closeboard and feather-edge?

Feather-edge is the board: a sawn board that is thicker on one edge than the other. Closeboard is the style of fence you build with it, overlapping those boards vertically on rails between posts. People use the two names for the same fence.

Do I need my neighbour's permission for a new fence?

If the fence is entirely on your side and yours to maintain, you do not need permission, but it is always worth a word, especially about which side faces them. If the deeds say the boundary is shared, the neighbour has a say and usually a share of the cost, so check before you commit.
