Fence repairs, or an honest word if it's time to replace

Snapped posts, blown panels and leaning runs, fixed properly rather than propped.

After a Scottish winter the phone rings with the same jobs: a post snapped at ground level, a panel blown out, a run of fence leaning into next door. Plenty of those can be put right for a fraction of the cost of a new fence, and we would rather tell you that than sell you a whole new boundary. Where the fence has reached the end of its life, with several posts gone and the boards soft, we will tell you that too, and price both, so you can decide.

Snapped and rotten posts

Timber posts almost always fail just at ground level, where they stay wet. The fix is to dig out the old footing and set a new post in concrete, or where the rest of the fence is in good shape, swap to a slotted concrete post so it never happens at that spot again. Propping a snapped post with a spur or a stake can buy time but it rarely lasts, and we will not dress it up as a proper repair. When one post has gone, we check its neighbours, because they are usually the same age and not far behind.

Blown panels and broken boards

A panel that has blown out of concrete posts can often go straight back in, with clips or wedges to stop it rattling, if the panel itself is sound. Split or broken closeboard boards can be replaced one at a time. Old woven and lap panels that have gone soft and brittle are not worth refitting; we will suggest replacing that run, often with closeboard built between the existing concrete posts if they are sound, which reuses the part of the fence that still has life in it.

Leaning fences

A fence leans because the posts have moved, either rotted, set too shallow or pushed by soil or roots behind them. Straightening it means freeing and resetting the posts, not tying it back. If the ground is higher on one side, for example where a neighbour has built a bed against it, the fence is acting as a retaining wall, which it was never meant to do; we can add a gravel board or low retaining board to take that load.

Repair or replace

As a rule of thumb, if more than a third of the posts are loose or rotten, or the boards crumble when you press a thumbnail in, a new fence is usually better value than repairs you will be paying for again next winter. If the concrete posts are sound, a new infill between them costs much less than a whole new fence. We will give you both figures in writing where both make sense.

How long it takes

Most repairs are done in a few hours; several posts can take a day.

Questions

Fence repairs: what people ask.

Can you replace just one fence post?

Yes. We dig out the old post and its footing and set a new one in concrete, then reattach the panels or rails. As a guide, a single post replacement runs about £95 to £108. We will tell you if the posts either side look ready to go too.

Who pays to fix a fence between two gardens?

In Scotland it depends on your title deeds. Many boundary fences, especially on older estates, are shared, and the cost of repair is split. If the deeds say the fence is yours, it is yours to maintain.

Is it worth repairing an old fence?

If the posts are mostly sound and only a section is damaged, usually yes. If a third or more of the posts are rotten or the boards are soft, a new fence is normally better value. We will give you both options in writing.
